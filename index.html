🌈 How is a rainbow made?

Does one raindrop make the whole rainbow? Or is it made by lots of raindrops working together? Let's play with the light and find out.

1 Follow light through ONE raindrop

Sunlight looks white, but it's secretly made of all the colors. When a ray hits a raindrop it bends going in (①), bounces off the back wall (②), and bends again coming out (③). It comes out heading mostly back toward the sun.

Tip: click or drag on the drop to move the ray. The graph shows the leaving angle for every place a ray can hit.

2 One drop, many directions, ONE color for you

Every color leaves the drop at a slightly different angle: red a little wider, violet a little narrower. So a single drop sends all the colors out, but in different directions. Where you stand decides which one reaches your eye. Move the eye around the drop!

3 Now add lots of drops

You're standing with the sun behind you, looking at falling rain. Each drop sends you just one color, and only if it sits at exactly the right angle from the point your shadow's head points to. Add drops and watch.

4 So why not lots of little rainbows?

Three more pictures answer that. First: what happens when drops stand one behind the other. Second: where the center of the rainbow is. Third: why it bends into an arc.

4a · Drops one behind the other

This is a map of the rain seen from above, with you on the left. Pick one line of sight. Every drop on that line, near or far, is at the same angle, so it sends you the same color. And in your eye they all land on the same spot. They don't build a new rainbow. They only make that spot brighter.

Try the "spread" slider: once the drops leave the line, each one is at its own angle, so each lands in its own place with its own color.

4b · Your head's shadow

Where is the middle of the rainbow? Stand with the sun behind you. A ray of sunlight skims past your head and hits the ground at the tip of your head's shadow. Looking along that line, away from the sun, you find the anti-solar point: the spot exactly opposite the sun. The rainbow is a ring of drops about 42° around that spot.

Lower sun, longer shadow, higher rainbow. The shadow's tip always sits at the center of the ring.

4c · Why is it an arc?

All the drops that send you red sit on the surface of a cone: your eye is the tip, the line toward your head's shadow is the axis, and the sides are 42° away from it. Seen from the tip, a cone is a circle. The ground simply cuts off the bottom, so you only see the top part: an arc.

Left: a 3D picture of the cone of red-to-violet light. Bright rings are above the ground, faded ones are hidden below it. Right: what you actually see.

5 Why do drops at the top and at the sides give the same color?

Look at the red edge of a rainbow: it runs across the top and down both sides. Those drops are in very different places. So why do they all send you red?

Two facts explain it. First, the sun is so far away that its light reaches every drop in the same direction, in parallel. Second, a drop is a little sphere, so it looks the same from every side. That means every drop does exactly the same thing: the ray bends, bounces and bends out, and the red leaves about 42° away from the line pointing back at the sun. Because a sphere is the same all the way around, the drop sends red out along a whole cone, not in just one direction.

The drop doesn't know where you are. If your eye happens to be on its cone, you get red from it. Below, three drops each have their own cone, and all three cones pass through your eye.

Each circle is one drop's cone of that color. The yellow lines are sunlight going in. The colored line is the ray that reaches your eye.

6 The magic angle: why 42° and not some other angle?

Rays that hit the drop at different heights leave at different angles. So a sunbeam hitting a drop sprays light in lots of directions. Most directions get only a thin trickle, which is the faint pale glow of the sky. But there's one special direction where the light piles up. Here is why.

6a · A narrow beam, in and out

Aim a thin beam at the drop and slide where it hits. Watch how wide the beam is when it leaves. The dots show where each ray lands.

6b · How bright is the light at each angle?

Now add up every ray that hits the drop and see how much light goes out at each angle. Each colored curve is one color of light. (This ignores wave effects, which soften the peaks a little.)

The peak is where the exit angle stops growing and turns around (the top of the curve in step 1). There, many different rays go the same way. The angle depends on how strongly the liquid bends light, so 42° is the number for water. A liquid that bends light more or less would make a rainbow at a different angle.

★ The short answer

  • One drop does NOT make a whole rainbow. It sends all its colors out, but each color goes a different way. You only catch one color from it.
  • The color you catch depends on the angle: about 42° from your shadow's direction is red, about 40° is violet, and the colors fall in between.
  • All drops at the same angle send you the same color, so they line up on a circle around the point your shadow points to. Red ring on the outside, violet inside, the rest in between. That's the rainbow.
  • A drop in front of or behind another one at the same angle doesn't make a second rainbow. It adds to the same ring, and the rainbow gets brighter.
  • That's why a rainbow isn't a thing in one place. It's a pattern of angles. You can never walk up to it, it moves when you move, and your friend standing next to you is looking at a slightly different rainbow made by different drops.
  • Try the sun-height slider: when the sun is higher than about 42°, the ring is below the horizon and you can't see it from the ground.
  • Bonus: a faint second rainbow appears when light bounces twice inside a drop. It comes out at about 51°, with the colors upside down.
  • Top or side, same color: every drop is a little sphere lit by parallel sunlight, so every drop does the same thing and sends red along a cone around the line back to the sun. Your eye sits on the cone of every drop on the ring.
  • Why 42°: rays that hit at different heights leave at different angles, but the exit angle stops growing at a maximum. Near that maximum, lots of rays leave in almost the same direction, so the light piles up and shines. The maximum for water is about 42°.
